What's probably behind your bathroom walls

Denver's housing goes from 1890s Victorians to new-build townhomes. The age of the house predicts most of the surprises a contractor will find after demo.

Pre-war bungalows and Denver squares

Common in Highland, Baker, Washington Park and Congress Park. Expect cast-iron drain lines, possibly galvanized steel supply pipe, plaster walls, and small bathrooms where a tub is tucked into a 5-foot alcove. Budget for drain and supply replacement while the walls are open.

Post-war ranches and split-levels

Common in Harvey Park, Virginia Village and much of southeast Denver. Often original tile over a mortar bed, a single bath plus a half bath, and limited exhaust. Adding a properly ducted fan is one of the best upgrades.

1990s and newer

Common in Central Park, Green Valley Ranch and newer infill. Usually plastic plumbing and better venting, so remodels run closer to quote. Watch for fiberglass tub-shower units that are cheap to replace but hard to remove in one piece.

These are typical patterns, not guarantees. Your contractor should confirm after looking at the house.

Final word belongs to Denver Community Planning and Development. Read its residential interior remodel page before you sign. Colorado has no statewide general contractor license, so check licensing with the city.

Built before 1978? The lead-safe rule applies

A large share of Denver's older neighborhoods were built before 1978, when lead paint was banned for homes. Under the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule, any contractor you pay to disturb painted surfaces in a pre-1978 home must be an EPA lead-safe certified firm and contain dust during demo.

  • Ask every bidder for their EPA RRP firm certificate number.
  • Expect plastic sheeting, sealed doorways, and HEPA cleanup in the bid.
  • If young children or a pregnant person live in the home, ask about cleaning verification at the end.

Claim the Denver Water toilet rebate

If you get a Denver Water bill, a new WaterSense toilet averaging 0.8 gallons per flush or less can earn a rebate of up to $100. Pick the model from Denver Water's list before you buy, keep the receipt, and apply after installation. Details: Denver Water toilet rebates.

Denver bath remodel FAQ

Do I need a permit to remodel a bathroom in Denver?

Cosmetic work such as paint or swapping a vanity in place usually does not. Moving plumbing, adding circuits, changing walls, or adding a vent fan usually does. Denver Community Planning and Development makes the call, so check its residential interior remodel page before work starts.

Can a Denver homeowner pull their own bathroom permit?

Denver has a homeowner permit process for people doing work on the home they live in, and Colorado's State Plumbing Board allows homeowners to do plumbing in their own residence with a permit. If you hire a contractor, the contractor should pull the permit.

My Denver house was built before 1978. Does that matter?

Yes. Under the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule, a paid contractor disturbing painted surfaces in a pre-1978 home must be an EPA lead-safe certified firm and follow lead-safe work practices.

Is there a Denver Water rebate for a new toilet?

Denver Water lists a rebate of up to $100 for a WaterSense toilet that averages 0.8 gallons per flush or less, for its customers. Check its rebate page for the current qualifying models before you buy.
