A bathroom remodel is the one time in a house's life when the supply pipes, the drain and the walls around them are all exposed at once. In a 1920s bungalow in Sunnyside or a 1940s brick home in Park Hill, that is also when you find out what the original builder used. Knowing the likely answer before demo day keeps the change orders small and the drinking water question settled.

Does my Denver house have a lead service line?

The service line is the pipe that carries water from the main under the street into your house. In Denver Water's territory the customer owns it, which is why the utility had to set up a dedicated program to replace the lead ones. Denver Water estimates that 60,000 to 64,000 properties in its service area may have lead service lines, and it is replacing them over a 15-year Lead Reduction Program that began in 2020.

Progress has been steady. Denver Water reported 10,308 lines replaced in the program's first two years, and by November 2024 Inside Climate News reported the utility was about halfway through the nearly 65,000 lines it had identified. Replacements are scheduled neighborhood by neighborhood, with the highest-risk areas and planned street work first.

How to find out about your line

  • Search your address on Denver Water's lead map. It shows your property's risk of lead in drinking water.
  • Ask for a free test. Denver Water offers free lead tests for homes with up to four units. A result under 1 microgram per liter suggests the line and plumbing are unlikely to contain lead.
  • Look at the pipe where it enters the house, usually near the meter or main shutoff in the basement. EPA researchers describe lead pipe as soft: a key or coin scratch shows shiny silver, and a magnet will not stick. A magnet does stick to galvanized steel.

If your line is known, possible or suspected lead, Denver Water's filter program sends a pitcher and filters certified to NSF Standard 53 for lead at no direct charge, with replacement cartridges about every six months until six months after the line is replaced. The utility says to use filtered water for drinking, cooking and preparing infant formula.

Should I replace the lead line myself during the remodel?

You can, but most owners are better off letting Denver Water's program do it. If you hire your own plumber to replace the line, Denver Water offers a one-time reimbursement of $3,800 that covers some of the total cost, and your application has to be approved before the work starts. The utility also asks owners to review its Redevelopment Policy first.

Whichever way it goes, two habits help. Send any replacement or remodel records to Denver Water so they can be added to your account, at 303-893-2444 or lead@denverwater.org. And after a replacement or any long shutoff, flush the lines: Denver Water suggests running the cold kitchen or bathroom tap for five minutes when water has been sitting for a few hours. Boiling water does not remove lead.

Timing tip: if your block is already on Denver Water's schedule, ask your remodeler to plan the bathroom's supply connections around that date rather than before it.

Why does galvanized pipe matter in an old Denver bathroom?

Galvanized pipe is steel coated with zinc. It was a common supply pipe in older American homes, and it corrodes from the inside over decades, which is why old bathrooms often have weak pressure at the shower or rust-tinted water after a vacation. It is also part of the lead story. Under the EPA's 2024 Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, a galvanized service line that is or ever was downstream of a lead service line is classed as "galvanized requiring replacement," and water systems must replace lead and those galvanized lines under their control within 10 years.

That federal rule covers service lines, not the pipes inside your walls. Still, if your home had a lead line and still has original galvanized pipe indoors, the remodel is a sensible time to talk with a plumber about replacing the bathroom's supply runs back to a clean connection. It is far cheaper to do while the wall is open than after the new tile is up.

What about old cast-iron drains?

Many pre-war Denver homes still drain through cast-iron pipe. It is heavy and quiet, and sections in good shape can stay. The trouble spots are usually the horizontal runs under the floor and the joint where a newer plastic section meets the old iron. Before a new shower pan or walk-in shower goes in, ask the plumber to run a camera down the drain while the floor is open. It takes little time and tells you whether you are replacing a short section or chasing a problem toward the main stack.

Any drain or supply change counts as plumbing work, so it falls under the permit rules covered in our guide to Denver bathroom remodel permits. In pre-1978 homes, cutting into painted plaster to reach those pipes also triggers the lead-safe work practices described on our main page.

What should an old-house bathroom quote include?

Hidden plumbing is the reason old-house remodels run over budget. A good quote does not pretend it can see inside the walls, but it does tell you how surprises will be priced. Ask every bidder to write these down:

  • Your service line status from Denver Water's map, and whether the work will be coordinated with a scheduled replacement.
  • What supply pipe they expect to find, and a per-run price if galvanized pipe has to be replaced back to the main line.
  • Whether the drain will be camera-inspected, and how replacing a cast-iron section will be priced.
  • Lead-free faucets, valves and fittings, and how joints will be made.
  • For pre-1978 homes, the firm's EPA lead-safe certification and the containment plan.
  • Which permits they will pull, by trade.

Old Denver house plumbing FAQ

How do I know if my Denver home has a lead service line?

Search your address on Denver Water's lead map, which shows your property's risk. Homes built before 1951 are more likely to have one. You can also request Denver Water's free lead test for homes with up to four units.

Does Denver Water charge to replace a lead service line?

No. Denver Water replaces customer-owned lead service lines with copper at no direct charge to the customer, scheduled neighborhood by neighborhood. If you replace the line yourself, a one-time $3,800 reimbursement covers part of the cost, with approval required before work starts.

Should I replace galvanized pipes during a bathroom remodel?

It is worth discussing with a plumber while the walls are open, especially if the home ever had a lead service line. The EPA classes galvanized service lines that are or were downstream of a lead line as needing replacement. Replacing old supply runs is far easier before new tile goes up.

Are old bathroom faucets a lead risk?

They can be. The EPA says that in homes without a lead service line, the most common problem is brass or chrome-plated brass faucets and plumbing with lead solder. Federal law now caps lead in new pipes and fixtures at a weighted average of 0.25 percent of the surfaces that touch water.

Does boiling water remove lead?

No. Denver Water says boiling does not remove lead. Use a filter certified to NSF Standard 53 for lead, and run the cold tap for five minutes when water has sat for a few hours.
